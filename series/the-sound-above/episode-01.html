The Core Question: How Did the First Room Come Together?

In this conversation, recorded on-site at the 2011 Software Craftsmanship North America conference, Sergio Pereira and I talk about the origins of Chicago Alt.NET.

Chicago Alt.NET was the very first technical user group I ever attended, and the very first stage where I ever gave a talk. Meeting on the second Wednesday of every month at Redpoint Technologies in the Sears Tower, Sergio created an open, supportive room for developers who wanted to build software with automated testing, continuous integration, and open source libraries at a time when enterprise IT culture was resistant to outside ideas.

What stands out watching this back today is how much the room was about welcoming people who felt isolated in their daily jobs. It was not about corporate certifications or selling tools. It was about sharing what we were learning after hours and giving developers the courage to bring better practices back to their teams on Monday morning.

The Alt.NET Manifesto, The Austin Open Spaces, and The Sears Tower Room

To understand why Sergio Pereira's Chicago Alt.NET meetup mattered, one has to revisit the landscape of 2007. In April of that year, David Laribee published the Alt.NET Manifesto, formulating a declaration of professional autonomy inside what was then a closed, vendor-dominated Microsoft developer ecosystem. Laribee outlined four core tenets:

  1. Platform as a tool, not a religion: You utilize the Microsoft platform as a practical tool rather than a theological boundary.
  2. Recognizing multiple sources of insight: You recognize that Microsoft is not the only source of great ideas, libraries, and frameworks.
  3. Uncompromising commitment to craft: You refuse to compromise on quality, agility, or craftsmanship simply because the default vendor toolchain does not support it out of the box.
  4. Proactive cross-ecosystem learning: You actively pursue learning, testing, and continuous feedback across language, runtime, and community boundaries.

The momentum catalyzed rapidly. In October 2007, the first Alt.NET Open Spaces conference convened in Austin, Texas. Martin Fowler documented the gathering in his Bliki, observing that Alt.NET represented a vital movement of developers refusing to accept whatever Redmond handed down, seeking inspiration from Smalltalk, Java, Ruby, and agile XP practices. By March 2008, Jeremy D. Miller authored the landmark "What is Alt.NET?" column in MSDN Magazine, bringing automated unit testing, IoC containers (StructureMap, Castle Windsor), NHibernate, and agile delivery directly into mainstream enterprise awareness.

This was not merely an academic debate: it was an active rebellion for software quality. As Richard Banks recalled in his retrospective, the community famously organized the Entity Framework v1 Vote of No Confidence, rejecting drag-and-drop design canvases in favor of Persistence Ignorance, POCO objects, and true testability. As Jeremy D. Miller noted in his 2019 retrospective, Alt.NET succeeded completely: it permanently shifted Microsoft from proprietary walled gardens to open source repositories, package management via NuGet, and the modern cross-platform .NET runtime.

Sergio Pereira carried this exact fire to Chicago. Every second Wednesday at Redpoint Technologies high inside the Sears Tower, developers gathered in a room where anyone could present and learn. That Sears Tower conference room became the nursery for Chicago's craftsmanship community, leading directly into ChicagoRuby, Software Craftsmanship McHenry County (SCMC), 8th Light, Obtiva, and the SCNA conferences.

Looking Back to Look Forward: Carrying Knowledge Across the Crisis

In Isaac Asimov's Foundation, Hari Seldon foresaw that the dark age between civilizational eras could be dramatically shortened if the core knowledge was deliberately gathered and stewarded by practitioners who understood how things actually worked.

As software engineering changes with AI code synthesis and autonomous developer tools, we face an interregnum of understanding: when syntax generation becomes effortless, the foundational muscle memory of debugging, system boundaries, and root-cause analysis risks atrophying.

The Alt.NET community taught us how to evaluate new technologies with an independent eye, how to rely on test suites and verified feedback loops, and how to build local peer support systems that outlast commercial hype cycles. We preserve these conversations not for nostalgia, but as an empirical manual for how human engineers maintain their agency and craft so that when the hype cycle cools, the knowledge to build durable platforms remains unbroken.
