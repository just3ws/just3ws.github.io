Why I Started Here

Sergio Pereira was my first UGtastic interview. On November 19, 2011, in a corridor at the Swissotel in downtown Chicago during SCNA 2011, I did not have a studio camera or an external audio rig. I sat down with Sergio, flipped open the lid of my 15-inch unibody MacBook Pro, opened Photo Booth, and hit record.

There is a personal reason I asked Sergio to sit down first. Chicago Alt.NET was the first user group where I ever stood up and gave a presentation. Going back through my blog posts from August 2008 surfaced a detail that connects those two moments. My first Chicago Alt.NET meeting was Wednesday, August 13, 2008. I took the Metra Union Pacific Northwest train into the city from Crystal Lake to see Robert Norton demonstrate ThoughtWorks Studios' Cruise continuous integration server. The person who recorded, edited, and posted the video of that meeting online, including my questions from the room, was Sergio Pereira. Three years later at SCNA, we traded roles and I recorded him.

In the interview, Sergio explains that one of Chicago Alt.NET's core goals was cultivating speakers from inside the group. Because the monthly meetings at Redpoint Technologies in the Sears Tower typically drew about twenty-five people, members could bring talks from work without facing an intimidating auditorium. Just three months after my first meeting, on November 12, 2008, I stood up in that room and gave my first user group talk on the NHaml view engine alongside Adam Tybor presenting on Spark.

What Recent Archival Discovery Added

Revisiting this fourteen-minute recording alongside the primary-source records recovered across 2008 to 2012 connects four threads that enrich the tape:

  • Logistics as Community Design: Sergio notes that meeting on the second Wednesday at 6:30 PM at Redpoint Technologies in the Sears Tower worked for downtown developers and suburban train commuters alike. Redpoint provided the room and sponsored the pizza every month. As explored in What the Interviews Noticed About Community Work, durable communities are built through practical operating decisions as much as shared beliefs.
  • Fishbowl Debates and Empirical Tests: At 11:42 on the tape, Sergio talks candidly about testing open projector nights and fishbowl discussions before settling into a rhythm curated with co-organizer Eduardo Scoz. My March 12, 2009 meeting recap captures one of those four-chair Fishbowl nights where Adam Tybor led a debate on Domain-Driven Design and Sergio held a circle discussion on the db4o object database. That same spring, a friendly Twitter debate with Sergio over whether retweeting Scott Hanselman reached unique followers prompted me to build twatio.com on ASP.NET MVC to measure audience overlap empirically. Years later, twatio.com redirected to ugtastic.com.
  • Cross-Platform Practice at Chicago Code Camp (just3ws/ccs): At 10:29 on the tape, Sergio says that in all of his community work, including Chicago Code Camp, he tried to keep the focus on software development rather than exclusively .NET. When we resurrected the 2010 to 2012 Ruby on Rails codebase for Chicago Code Camp and Code Camp Server (just3ws/ccs) and checked the Software Craftsmanship Manifesto Signatory Network Map, the evidence matched his words directly. Sergio signed the Manifesto in 2009 as Signatory #592 from Grayslake, Illinois, where Chicago Code Camp met at the College of Lake County. He spoke at the May 2012 camp and was the number two code contributor to our Ruby 1.9.3, Rails 3.0, Haml, and Sass repository with 24 commits alongside my 764 commits.
  • From Photo Booth and Five Raspberry Pis to Searchable Dialogue: For years after 2011, transcribing more than two hundred interviews was out of reach. My 2014 crowdsourcing attempt (many-hands-make-light-work) stalled because commercial speech APIs were too expensive and manual transcription could not scale. Learning local whisper.cpp orchestration on five Raspberry Pis in 2023 (The Fluffle) and moving to Apple Silicon transcription today lets us search every turn of Sergio's conversation and link it directly to the 2008 Wayback captures and 2012 git commits.

What to Listen For

Sergio refuses to define Alt.NET as a fixed doctrine. Instead, he describes a room where working .NET developers could see test-driven development, behavior-driven development, NHibernate, CodeSharp, IronRuby, and Kanban, then decide for themselves what worked. Key moments across the recording include:

  • Continuous Improvement Over Labels: Why Sergio avoids a rigid definition of Alt.NET and focuses on improving software quality and developer practices beyond a single vendor.
  • The Second Wednesday at 6:30 PM: Meeting monthly at Redpoint Technologies in the Sears Tower on a schedule that worked for both city developers and suburban train commuters. (Aug 2008 Post)
  • A Room of Twenty-Five and Cultivating Speakers: Why a twenty-five-person room was the right size to encourage team developers to give their first user group talk. (Nov 2008 Wayback)
  • Late 2007 Origins and Platform Evolution: My thank-you to Sergio for the room where I gave my first user group talk, followed by Sergio's recollection of launching the group in late 2007 with Derik Whittaker and Ryan Rinaldi and watching Microsoft adopt ASP.NET MVC and jQuery.
  • Seeing TDD and BDD in Action (Line 145): Giving everyday developers a place to see ideas from other languages and judge the evidence for themselves. (Interactive Transcript)
  • Cross-Platform Regulars, Kanban & Chicago Code Camp: How non-.NET developers joined for topics like Kanban, and how Sergio carried that multi-platform approach into Chicago Code Camp. (just3ws/ccs · Signatory Map)
  • Redpoint's Pizza, Fishbowls & Curation with Eduardo Scoz: What worked and what failed when testing open projector nights and fishbowl debates versus curating speakers with Eduardo Scoz, setting up Episode 2 (Ray Hightower & ChicagoRuby). (Mar 2009 Fishbowl)

The present-day echo is modest and practical. In 2008, the default vendor path was drag-and-drop WebForms wizards that hid HTTP and made testing difficult; today, it is often generative AI tools that produce plausible code without exposing domain boundaries. Engineers still benefit from small, trusted rooms where people share real work, look across boundaries, and verify new tools together.
